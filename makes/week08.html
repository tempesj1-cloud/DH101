The Artifact

I mapped my own digital life (Option C) as a network of 27 nodes and 33 connections. It shows which apps collect which kinds of data about me, who owns those apps, and who else ends up seeing the data. Four line styles mark four relationships (collects, owned by, shares data with, alternative to), node size is the number of connections, and node shape marks the column.

Network Analysis

What is the most central node? Why?

It depends on the measure. Instagram has the most connections (6): it collects three kinds of data about me, belongs to Meta, and is an alternative to Snapchat and Pinterest. But Contacts & social graph has the highest betweenness (0.24), meaning about a quarter of the shortest paths between other nodes run through it. Five apps (Instagram, Snapchat, Gmail, LinkedIn, and Venmo) all collect it, so it ties my social, work, email, and payment lives together. Instagram is the busiest app, but my contacts are the real bridge.

What clusters emerged?

The graph splits into three pieces. The main piece (20 nodes) holds a Google group (Gmail, YouTube, Maps, Alphabet) and a social-and-ads group (Instagram, Snapchat, Pinterest, Meta, and advertisers). Pinterest also links that ads group to Amazon Associates. The other two pieces are islands: Hudl with my game film and the coaches who see it, and Claude with my prompts and Anthropic. Neither touches the main graph.

What was surprising?

Three things. Contacts outrank Instagram on betweenness. Advertisers & data brokers receives four lines, yet it is the one node I never choose or use. And the two islands show that the data I create for football and for AI work stays separate from everything else I do online, at least in what I could find.

What is MISSING?

Nine of the 27 nodes have only one connection. They are endpoints because my edge list stops there, not because nothing more happens. Advertisers & data brokers is one box standing in for many companies I cannot name. I also left out my phone’s operating system, my carrier, my bank, and school systems. And a line shows that a connection exists, not how much data moves along it.

Artist Statement

What does my network reveal about power?

Power on this graph sits where lines converge, not where they start. Four lines end at Advertisers & data brokers, and Alphabet owns three of my eleven apps, so Gmail, YouTube, and Maps all feed one company. I chose which apps to map, but not who sits behind them. The graph shows that my choices stop at the app column, and everything to the right of it happens without me.

Who is visible? Who is invisible?

Visible: the apps I tap every day and the big names that own them, because they have logos and privacy policies. Invisible: the advertisers and data brokers, who appear as one yellow box because I did not name a single one. The format forced that. A node needs a name, and the people who buy information about me do not give one. I am invisible too. I am not a node, only the data on the left.

How would a different structure tell a different story?

Same 33 rows, different drawing. With owners in the center, Alphabet and Meta would look like the hubs of my life and my apps would look like their doors. With me in the center, it would look like a personal toolkit that I control. Like football film, the same play looks different from another camera angle. My columns tell a story of a trail: data leaves me on the left and ends up with others on the right.

Accessibility Statement

This site was built by Jake Tempesta for DH 101.

I have tried to make it accessible by:

  • Adding skip links at the top so keyboard users can jump to the text list of connections or the written analysis.
  • Using a clear heading order, page landmarks, and a nav bar that links to every section.
  • Including a full text version of the network graph (the "All connections as text" table, with labeled headers) so screen readers can read every connection.
  • Letting keyboard users Tab to a node in the graph and press Enter to select it.
  • Not relying on color alone: line types also differ by dash pattern, and node shapes differ by column.
  • Adding visible focus outlines and respecting the reduced-motion setting.
  • Labeling buttons and live regions for screen readers, and adding a message for anyone with JavaScript turned off.
  • Setting the page language to English and making the layout work on a phone.
