    The Generator

    Coach Speak Bot generates the kind of thing a defensive line coach yells in the film room or at the sled. Hit “Blow the whistle” for one yell or “Run 10 reps” for ten. Every yell lands in a film log on the page.

    The grammar has 13 symbols. Each one is a list Tracery picks from at random:

    • origin: 8 sentence templates that set the shape of each yell
    • opener: how the coach stops the tape (“Stop the tape,” “Whoa whoa whoa”)
    • name: what he calls you (“Big Man,” “Hoss,” “Freshman”)
    • critique / critique2: what you did wrong (“your get-off was a full business day late”)
    • comparison: what you looked like (“a lawn chair in a windstorm”)
    • fix: the real coaching point (“Get your hands inside,” “Low man wins”)
    • wisdom: sideline philosophy (“Pad level is a lifestyle”)
    • skill: what the comparison beat you at (“get-off,” “pursuit angles”)
    • punishment + count: what it costs you (“fifteen up-downs”)
    • rating: his grade for the rep (“soft as a dinner roll”)
    • closer: how it ends (“Again!”, “Do it right or do it twice.”)

    The full grammar JSON is below and can be pasted straight into the Tracery editor at tracery.io.

    How the bot works (full grammar)

    Every yell starts from one of 8 sentence templates in origin. Tracery swaps each #symbol# for a random entry from that list, so one template can produce thousands of different yells.

    What I Rejected
    
  
    I kept 8 of 120, so 112 got cut. Most fell into four groups:
    
  
      
    
    • Broken grammar (batch 1). The whole first batch had lowercase sentences after “!” or “.”, like “Bubba! you’re playing with your eyes closed.” That bug is why I made a second version of the grammar.
      • 
    
    • Comparisons that don’t match the mistake. “I’ve seen a parked car with better eyes” doesn’t mean anything. “You took a false step. You look like a sumo wrestler at a ballet recital” is funny alone but has nothing to do with a false step.
      • 
    
    • Clunky stacking. “That’s ten laps around the practice field after practice” says practice twice. “A hundred laps around the practice field” is so extreme it stops sounding like a coach.
      • 
    
    • Repeats. Batch 2 produced the exact same grandmother yell twice (#21 and #47), and “Nope. Pad level is a lifestyle.” opened two outputs in a row in batch 1. By the second batch the same jokes kept cycling back, which is when I stopped.
      • 
  
    

    


    
  
    Process Documentation
    
  
    I picked Track A and built a text generator in Tracery, a tool that fills sentence templates with random words from lists you write. The idea came from the film room. I play defensive line at Denison, and D-line coaches have their own language: pad level, get-off, hands inside, and a comparison for every bad rep.
    
  
    I built it with Claude. I picked the concept from three options, and Claude wrote the grammar as JSON and the code for the bot’s web page. The grammar has 13 symbols. The origin symbol holds 8 sentence templates, and each template pulls from lists like name, critique, comparison, fix, and closer. Some symbols nest inside others, so punishment calls count to make “fifteen up-downs.”
    
  
    The parameters I controlled were the templates, what goes in each list, and how many options each list has. The key choice was keeping the critiques and fixes real, so every yell has a correct coaching point, and putting the absurd stuff in comparison and rating. That split is what makes the outputs work. The batches used a fixed random seed, so they can be regenerated exactly.
    
  
    I generated 120 outputs total in two batches of 60. The first batch had a capitalization bug: when a critique started a sentence, it stayed lowercase. We fixed the templates with Tracery’s capitalize modifier and ran a second batch. I curated only from batch 2, but I kept batch 1 because the bug is part of the process.
    
  
    Curation started with Claude sorting the outputs against my test: would a coach actually say the first half, and is the second half funny? Then I went through the picks myself and checked the football. Last, the bot went on a live page with a whistle button and a film log, so anyone can run their own reps.
    

    


    
  
    Artist Statement
    
  
    Coach Speak Bot can yell forever. But within 60 yells it repeated one word for word, and by the second batch I could see the same jokes cycling back. That’s the exhaustion of novelty. Infinite output doesn’t mean infinite surprise, and once the surprises stopped, generating more felt pointless.
    
  
    So where is the human? In my process the creative decisions came at three points. First, the concept: coach speak, because it’s a language I hear every day at practice. Second, the split in the grammar: keep the coaching points real and let the comparisons get absurd. Third, the curation, deciding which outputs were actually funny and which were just random.
    
  
    That last part needed someone who plays. The bot has no idea that high pad level means standing up tall and slow, so it can’t know the grandmother joke fits. It also can’t tell that “a sumo wrestler at a ballet recital” has nothing to do with a false step. I can. My criteria were simple: is the football right, would a coach say it, and does it get a laugh in the room.
    
  
    If someone saw my 8 picks without explanation, I think they’d guess a person chose them, because they all sound like the same coach. “Pure” AI creativity would be the raw list of 120. “Pure” human creativity would be a real coach yelling at me after a bad rep. My work sits in the middle, closer to the coach.
